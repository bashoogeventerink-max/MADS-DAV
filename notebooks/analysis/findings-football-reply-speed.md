# Findings — football conversations and reply speed (Analysis 18)

## Reading the chart

- Dot = one conversation; black point = median of the group; bar = 95% range
  of that median (resampling days).

## Data

- 1,052 conversations of 5+ messages (a new conversation starts after 401 min
  of silence), group chat 2020–2026, 9 people.
- Football message = matches a list of clubs, players, countries, football
  words and scores.

## Limits

- The list finds ~60% of football talk, and ~96% of its hits are football.
  Misses land in '0', which shrinks the gap.
- A single football message ('1') is not clearly faster.

## Checks

- Same conversation length: 2+ is ~40% faster (95% range 14–58%), i.e. about
  a third faster (the old subtitle's second half).
- Without NL match days: 42%. Without tournaments: 51%.
- Shuffle test: football labels shuffled within length deciles — 1 in 1,000
  shuffles as fast.
- With time of day and weekend: 28%.
- Weaker and uncertain in 2023–26 (19%).

## Reflection

When the conversation has two or more messages associated with football, people respond five times as fast compared to conversations without any football messages. The median response time for conversations without any football messages is 8.6 minutes, whilst if the conversation has two or more messages related to football, this number drops to 1.6 minutes. This might be related to most people in the group being football fans.

The decline is seen first: the continuous line from left to right guides the eye (continuity), and the black line and points stand out through contrast against the faint dots in the background. Next, the orange dots mark the conversations about football, set apart by colour from the light-blue ones without football (similarity); with one colour, the small football groups would get lost among the others. The line between the medians uses the connection principle and shows reply time going down step by step as football messages increase. This is allowed because the x-axis has a natural order (0 → 1 → 2+); between categories without an order, a line would suggest a trend that only exists because of the order chosen. Finally, grouping the dots in columns (proximity) lets the reader see each column as conversations with the same number of football messages.

The subtitle and the point labels show the same numbers for different readers: the subtitle gives the comparison to the reader who scans, while the point labels help the reader who studies the chart, since the steps on a log axis are not equal and exact values are hard to read. The annotation for the dot, point and bar stays on the chart because it explains the marks intuitively without taking much space. The length check, which confirms the claim also holds at the same conversation length, is described in the text rather than on the chart to keep the chart free of clutter.

To test the claim, I looked at alternative explanations. At the same conversation length, people still reply 41% faster in conversations with two or more football messages (95% range 14–58%), and without NL match days (42%) or international tournaments (51%) the effect doesn't shrink, so it isn't just 'we're all watching the same match'. In a shuffle test I randomly swapped the football labels between conversations of similar length, 1,000 times; only 1 shuffle made the 2+ group as fast as the real data, so chance is a very unlikely explanation. The biggest weakness is when a conversation starts: taking time of day and weekend into account, the effect drops from 41% to 28%, with a range that almost reaches 0%, so part of the difference is about when football is discussed, not only that it is. On the other hand, my football list finds only about 60% of football messages; the missed ones land in "0" and, if they are just as fast, make that group look faster, so the 41% is probably an underestimate. Finally, with only 26 football conversations in 2023–26 (range −52% to +32%), the effect can't be shown clearly for recent years, which is not the same as showing there is no effect.
